03.  Treating your AI like a software developer working for you. 10 min
Listen to this section:
(a vibe coders meet up!)
When you're building software using AI, it's helpful to shift your mindset a bit. Instead of thinking of 
your AI assistant as a simple tool, consider it more like a developer you've just hired. Imagine you're 
responsible for managing a software development project in the real world, how would you approach 
it? Y ou wouldn't simply say, "Hey, build me an app that tells me what to wear based on a photo of my 
wardrobe," and expect flawless results.  Sure, you can "vibe-code" away with a prompt like that, but 
ultimately, if that's all the input you're going to do, you're going to run into serious issues.   Instead, 
you should go through a thoughtful process, carefully outlining your requirements, gathering input 
from different stakeholders, and continuously refining your project as you go along.
In a real-world scenario, imagine you're tasked with developing a new e-commerce platform. Before 
you write even a single line of code, you'd first gather input from various stakeholders to ensure the 
software you're building aligns with their expectations and needs. Each stakeholder typically has 
distinct areas of interest and priorities. 
• For instance, the business team might focus on core functionalities and revenue-generating 
features, such as seamless checkout experiences, robust inventory management, and 
integrated payment systems. They will want features that directly impact profitability, ease of 
use, and customer retention.
• Meanwhile, the marketing team would likely emphasize user acquisition strategies, user 
experience, branding consistency, analytics, and integration with social media channels. They’d 
be concerned with how easily they can track user behavior, run promotional campaigns, and 
convert visitors into paying customers.
• On the other hand, your finance team might focus on cost efficiency, budgeting, financial 
reporting capabilities, and scalable infrastructure. They will ask questions like, “How much will 
hosting cost?”, “Are there more cost-effective solutions?”, or “How do transaction fees affect our 
margins?” These considerations can directly impact which technologies you choose and how 
you design your platform to manage expenses effectively.
• Finally, your operations and infrastructure team would likely emphasize reliability, scalability, and 
security. Their priority would be ensuring that the app can handle peak traffic during sales or 
promotional events, that it’s safe against cyber threats, and that customer data is managed 
securely and compliantly.
Each of these stakeholders' interests shapes the final form of your software significantly. By 
thoughtfully incorporating their insights, you ensure the resulting application is not just functional, but 
strategically aligned with your broader business goals, financially viable, and operationally sound. 
The more comprehensive your stakeholder engagement at this stage, the clearer your instructions 
become, and consequently, the higher quality of code and output you will receive from your AI 
assistant.

Here's the best part, you can simply ask the LLM to play the role of that stakeholder so they can

Now, bring that same disciplined approach into your interactions with an LLM. Rather than relying on

developer. Each prompt should build logically upon the previous one, detailing exactly what you're 
looking for and providing context whenever possible.
This approach leads you toward creating a Product Requirements Document (PRD)—a 
comprehensive guide that outlines every aspect of your software project. A well-crafted PRD includes 
user stories, feature descriptions, technical constraints, budget considerations, and deployment 
plans. When you're using an LLM, your PRD might begin as a simple series of structured prompts 
and gradually expand into a robust set of instructions that guide the AI to generate higher-quality, 
more accurate code.  
Consider an example: Mike, a small-business consultant, wanted an app to streamline appointment 
scheduling. Instead of simply prompting, "Make me an appointment app so that customers can make 
appointments with me by going to a web page,"  That's the bare minimum to get started, but you 
won't get very far with that. 
Trying a different way:  Mike approached his LLM with a detailed vision. He first described the core 
functionalities clearly: an easy way for customers to create appointments, calendar integration, 
automatic reminders via email and SMS, basic customer information, and payment integration. Then, 
he refined his prompts based on specific feedback from test users and colleagues, just like he would 
when working with a traditional development team. The end result? A basic app to begin with, that he 
could build upon in stages that would add one feature at a time getting to the overall desired goal.
Y ou just can't build this all in one prompt and expect something amazing.  Y ou need to break it 
into phases, and add features one by one, making sure the app works well through the entire 
build process.
The key takeaway here is that the more precise, structured, and thoughtful your instructions, the 
better your results. Treat your LLM as if it were your employee—give it clear guidance, context, and 
iterative feedback—and you'll be amazed at what you can build together.

04.  What is “good” software? 11 min
Listen to this section:
Before you dive deeper into building your project, let’s pause for a moment and consider something 
fundamental: what exactly is "good" software? Y ou might initially think it’s software that simply works 
without errors—and that’s certainly important—but truly good software goes much deeper than 
functionality alone.
Think about apps or tools you use daily that you genuinely enjoy and rely upon. Maybe it's your 
favorite productivity app, streaming platform, or online shopping site. What sets these apart isn't just 
that they function; it's that they're intuitive, reliable, scalable, secure, and genuinely solve a real 
problem for users. They seamlessly fit into your life, making tasks easier and often even enjoyable.
When defining good software, start with the end-user in mind. A good application anticipates user 
needs and addresses pain points directly. For example, consider a budgeting app. A basic budgeting 
app might simply track income and expenses, and technically, it "works." But a great budgeting app 
goes beyond this—offering automated insights into spending habits, taking photos of receipts to input 
data rather than you typing anything, alerts for unusual transactions, and tailored recommendations 
for savings. It makes complex financial data understandable so the user knows what to do with that 
information.
To build that, you need to figure out several things:
• what language are you going to write it in?  (hint:  ask the AI for help on that)
• how does it need to be hosted? (hint: SPA (single page application), frontend/backend (with a 
database), storage, authentication (user accounts))
• how should users sign up?
• how much should it cost them?  (in other words, how will you make money?)
• can you add a freemium version?
• and other questions for your PRD
The user experience is only part of the picture. Good software must also be scalable meaning it 
performs equally well whether it's used by ten people or ten thousand. Imagine launching your app 
successfully only to discover it crashes under heavier user traffic. Scalability considerations from the 
start ensure your app can gracefully handle growth without sacrificing performance.
Security is another critical aspect. Users trust software with personal data, from basic contact 
information to sensitive financial details. Good software earns and maintains this trust through robust 
security practices. Think back to our budgeting app example: if users are going to trust your app with 
their spending habits and transaction history, it needs encryption, secure authentication, secure data 
storage, and regular security audits to protect their data comprehensively.  Furthermore, thanks to 
GDPR regulations in Europe, users needs to be able to either download their data, have a way to 
delete their account, and ultimately have control over their content.

Lastly, good software is maintainable and cost-effective. It should be structured clearly so future 
enhancements, fixes, or new features can be efficiently added without rewriting everything from 
scratch. Good software also carefully considers ongoing operational costs like hosting, third-party 
API integrations, and maintenance efforts. Poorly constructed software, by contrast, can become 
financially unsustainable over time.
If you don't know what's invovled, just ask the LLM!
We have upcoming course modules on scalability, security, and UX (user experience) on this platform 
that you will come to later to give you a great foundation.  However, it always helps to simply ask for 
more information.  Role play telling the AI to be a different person and get their assessment of things.  
The UI, the "flow" of an app, the security configuration, etc.  The information you need is all there, you 
just need to be asking the right questions to get the AI to do what you need it to as it won't always 
remember or feel inclined to tell you.
Let’s tie this back to your own project. As you work with your AI assistant, think beyond immediate 
functionality. Clarify these broader aspects of good software within your prompts and your overall 
plan. Provide guidance on user experience expectations, scalability requirements, security standards, 
and maintainability preferences. The clearer you define these qualities from the outset, the better 
your final product will be.  All this helps drive you towards building that PRD which will be the 
blueprint for your application.
Questions to consider:

05.  Get better results through better prompts 10 min
Listen to this section:
When working with AI to build software, the quality of your results is directly tied to how clearly and 
thoroughly you communicate your ideas. Imagine again that you're managing a software developer in 
real life. Y ou must provide specific guidance on exactly what needs improvement The same approach 
applies here. The more detailed and context-rich your prompts, the better the AI's output will be. So 
let’s dive deeper into how you can consistently write prompts that produce outstanding results.
Providing more context
Instead of "make the formatting look better", try, "On a mobile view, the page looks like it's been 
squashed a bit just below the hero section.  We need to add more spacing below it.  Here's a 
screenshot and I've drawn on it where we need to add more spacing. 
First off, never underestimate the power of context. The AI assistant you're working with doesn't have 
access to your environment or your full vision unless you clearly describe it. If you're encountering an 
error, don't just tell the AI, "My app isn't working right.  I get an error message" Instead, describe 
precisely what's happening: share the exact error messages, copy and paste code outputs, include 
screenshots with markup, or even capture video snippets demonstrating the issue in action. 
Check out the installation page (link also in the Resources box on the Dashboard) on how to enable 
the javascript console to see realtime logging data.
Providing screenshots (which you then drag and drop into the chat)
Let’s say you're developing a dashboard and the layout isn't rendering correctly. Instead of vaguely 
stating, "Dashboard looks weird," you might prompt the AI like this: "Here's a screenshot of my 
dashboard page. Notice the widgets overlap at certain screen sizes. Here’s the CSS and React 
component code for this page. How can you adjust this to make it responsive?" For UI-related 
queries, provide annotated screenshots showing exactly what needs to be changed or fixed. When 
you give this kind of detailed input, your AI assistant can diagnose the issue and generate solutions 
far more accurately.

Additionally, if you find yourself stuck on a tough problem, don't hesitate to ask the AI to provide 
multiple solutions or approaches. A prompt like, "Give me three ways to fix this database connection 
error, and rate each solution on your confidence level for fixing this specific issue," can be 
tremendously helpful. Y ou'll receive varied insights, allowing you to weigh your options carefully

Another valuable tactic is to proactively explore solutions or technologies you might not be aware of. 
If you're unsure about which cloud database platform to choose, simply ask something like, "What 
cloud-based databases are suitable for securely storing user data? Can you highlight their main 
features, pros, cons, and typical costs?" Similarly, you might ask, "Are there reliable free SMTP mail

hearing you need an SMTP server, then you could continue to ask for any free options.  These open-
ended prompts often uncover valuable new tools, resources, or solutions you hadn't considered.
Ultimately, the clarity, thoroughness, and precision of your prompts shape your success when using 
AI to develop software. Treat your interactions thoughtfully, like conversations with a trusted, yet junior 
developer who requires clear, detailed guidance. The effort invested here directly translates to higher-
quality, more robust, and reliable software.  More importantly, you will spend less time on your app as 
you will get more done as part of your conversation.

Best Practices for Good Prompt Engineering:
1. Provide Rich Context:Always try to include detailed descriptions, screenshots, error logs, or 
code outputs. The more complete your context, the more accurate the AI's response will be. The 
less requests you use getting what you want.
2. Break Down Complex Problems:Split big issues into smaller, manageable prompts. Solve each 
part methodically to avoid confusion and errors. Have the AI create a long task list of what's 
needed and methodically work your way through it.
3. Request Multiple Solutions:When stuck, ask your AI assistant explicitly for several possible 
solutions, along with confidence ratings, to help you evaluate options clearly.
4. Ask Open-ended Questions:If you're uncertain about available technologies or solutions, use 
prompts like, "What are the available options for...?" This can uncover new tools or methods you 
weren't aware of.
5. Pause and Reassess Regularly:If solutions repeatedly fail, explicitly prompt the AI to pause, 
reassess, and clarify. Provide clear observations, additional logs, or debugging data to help it 
find the root cause.
6. Use Specific Examples:Reference other websites, apps, or features directly in your prompts. 
This gives clear benchmarks or visual goals for your assistant to target.
7. Include Visuals with Annotations:Screenshots with clear markup or annotations dramatically 
enhance clarity, especially for UI/UX tasks. This can save you significan't hours wasted (and 
usage requests)

8. Confirm AI Understanding:Occasionally ask the AI to summarize your instructions back to you. 
This ensures alignment and catches misunderstandings early.
9. Explicitly Mention Constraints:Clarify limitations upfront, such as budget constraints, preferred 
technologies, or deadlines, to get more practical, relevant solutions.
10. Test and Validate Thoroughly:Always verify changes proposed by the AI. Carefully test major 
modifications to ensure no unintended side effects or regressions occur.

06.  Challenging Your AI and Improving Solutions 5 min
Listen to this section:
Continuing from our previous section on good prompts, let's talk about an essential strategy for 
getting the absolute best out of your AI assistant: actively challenging the solutions and ideas it 
provides. 
Just like working with a human developer, your AI assistant's suggestions might initially seem great 
but without deeper scrutiny and proactive questioning, they might miss important considerations. 
Encouraging your AI to critically evaluate both your ideas and its own proposals can uncover 
significant potential issues and lead to stronger, more robust solutions.
One common pitfall users face is getting trapped in a cycle of trying changes, finding they don't work, 
and either rolling them back, or leaving the code there but without gaining ground. This is common 
when you're trying to implement some major changes.  Or diganosing some security errors in the 
logs.  First off, just take a deep breath. 
Now, prompt the AI clearly: "I've tried these changes, and they're not working. Let’s stop and 
reassess. Can you help me figure out why these solutions haven't worked? Please suggest additional 
options and give me your confidence level in them." Encouraging the AI to stop and take stock helps 
break frustrating cycles and usually reveals deeper problems or simpler solutions.  
When you do fix your issue, you might introduce a new one, so in light of that it's crucial to remember 
that your AI assistant, while incredibly powerful, isn't flawless. AI can forget previous context, make 
assumptions, or occasionally introduce mistakes. It may sometimes remove essential code when 
editing or accidentally introduce bugs. Therefore, don't just blindly accept every suggestion. 
Thoroughly test each solution, especially major changes, to ensure nothing else has inadvertently 
broken. If something does go wrong, clearly communicate this back to your AI assistant, offering 
precise feedback so it learns to improve its next suggestions.
Here's another example:  Imagine you're building a user-authentication flow. The AI suggests altering 
your authentication logic. Don't immediately assume it's perfect—carefully test all scenarios: user 
registration, login, session management, error handling, and security checks. If anything fails, 
document the issue and explain precisely back to the AI: "Y our last edit caused new registrations to 
fail with this error message. Here's the detailed log output and related code. Can we carefully revisit 
and fix this?" This method ensures accuracy and reliability in your final product.

In a different app I built, I have a user dashboard.  In this app you submit a link to a social media 
video and the app processes it for information.  This processing with AI takes about 30-40 seconds.  
Y our AI assistant suggests implementing a "polling" system, where the dashboard repeatedly checks 
(every few seconds) to show if the data is ready and changes an icon from processing > completed. 
At first glance, this seems perfectly reasonable, especially during testing or development.
But here's where challenging your AI assistant becomes crucial. What happens when your app 
moves from testing to production, with potentially thousands - or even tens of thousands - of users 
simultaneously using this feature? While polling might work smoothly during your testing phase, it 
can create enormous performance bottlenecks and scalability issues in a real-world environment. 
Frequent polling requests from thousands of concurrent users could overload your server, degrade 
performance, and negatively impact the user experience.
In this scenario, it's essential to prompt the AI to recognize your application's scale requirements 
explicitly. Y ou might say something like, "Polling is great in a small test environment, but what about 
when we scale this to 10,000 simultaneous users? Could this approach cause performance issues, 
and what alternatives could better support this level of usage?" This kind of proactive questioning 
pushes your AI assistant to consider scalability concerns, prompting it to offer alternative solutions—
such as WebSockets or server-sent events (SSE), both of which can provide real-time updates more 
efficiently at scale.  I wasn't aware about WebSockets at the time, but it turned out to be a great 
suggestion to handle the volume.
Think about your mobile users
Another critical area to challenge is User Interface (UI) and User Experience (UX) design. Suppose 
the AI assistant suggests changes or additions to the desktop layout of your app. It’s important not to 
automatically assume these changes translate seamlessly to mobile devices. The AI might not 
automatically think about the mobile view unless explicitly instructed. Therefore, proactively ask, 
"How does this design change impact the mobile view? Can you ensure that this solution maintains 
usability and visual consistency across desktop and mobile devices?" This ensures your AI takes a 
more holistic and responsive approach to UI/UX design, helping you maintain a high-quality 
experience across all devices.
Obviously, you can test this yourself but you don't have every phone to use, just your own.  
Encouraging your AI assistant to critique its own proposals is equally valuable. Y ou could instruct it: 
"Here’s my idea: can you identify any potential problems or unintended consequences I might not 
have considered?" or "Y ou've proposed this approach; what are some potential weaknesses or 
scalability concerns with your solution?" By explicitly requesting this type of analysis, you not only 
uncover hidden issues early but also reinforce good development practices that emphasize 
thoroughness, anticipation of potential pitfalls, and strategic foresight.
Ultimately, challenging your LLM consistently pushes it beyond surface-level answers, guiding it 
toward more thoughtful, robust solutions. This practice of mutual critique and questioning not only 
builds better software but also sharpens your own strategic thinking and development skills.

Let's shift gears a bit and talk about Software as a Service, often called SaaS. This is software that 
users typically access online through a subscription model rather than buying it outright with a one-
time payment. Building a SaaS product is an exciting step and can be highly profitable, but it

08.  Just ship! Don’t waste time perfecting in the beginning 10 min
Listen to this section:
Here’s one of the biggest roadblocks for aspiring software builders: perfectionism. It’s incredibly 
tempting to keep polishing, tweaking, and fine-tuning your app until every pixel feels just right, 
especially when you’re building something you care deeply about.  As you build things, you realise it 
would be great to add this feature, add that feature.  Add something else shiny you saw on another 
website.  The list can be endless frankly!
But here's the truth: if you're the only person using it, you're not building a product, you're building a 
pet project.
Y ou don’t need to wait until your software is “finished” (spoiler: it never really is). What you need is to 
ship something. Something real. Something users can interact with. That’s how you find out whether 
your idea actually has legs by testing product/market fit in the real world. Until that happens, you’re 
operating on guesswork.  This very website was shipped well before all the modules were completed, 
and with a few missing features from the site.   It will also be ok though as it doesn't have to be 
perfect.
Instead of building the entire vision upfront, focus on developing a Minimum Viable Product (MVP). 
This is a lightweight version of your app that includes only the core functionality necessary for early 
users to experience the main value you’re offering. It’s your proof of concept, the spark that lights the 
fire.
Let’s say you’re building an AI-powered resume builder. Rather than adding every feature under the 
sun: job-specific templates, cover letters, portfolio links, analytics, etc. your MVP might focus on 
generating a single high-quality resume from user input. That alone is enough to get feedback, test 
your core premise, and prove demand. And once you see people getting value from that MVP , you’ll 
feel way more confident in continuing to build.
If you're seeking investors, getting to this point is also good to show them a working prototype.  It 
gives them a chance to get a look and feel for the soution and understand you're really building this 
and it's not just smoke in the air. 
At this stage, consider launching a beta program. Offer users early access in exchange for feedback 
or a reward, like discounted or free access once the platform officially launches. Reach out to 
potential users where they already hang out: industry forums, Discord groups, Reddit threads, Twitter 
(X), niche Facebook communities, even in-person meetups. Wherever they are, go there.
Y ou can also host live demos or book short intro calls with users. Walk them through the product and 
showcase what you’ve built so far. Not only does this help you gather valuable insights, but it shows 
your users that you’re actively listening and that you’re building something with them, not just for them.

09.  Patience is More Than a Virtue, It’s a Requirement 6 min
Listen to this section:
Let’s be real: this journey you’re on, building software with AI, especially if you're new to coding is 
going to test your patience. There will be moments of excitement and flow, yes, but there will also be 
days where it feels like you’re running in circles. One fix leads to another issue. A feature that worked 
yesterday mysteriously breaks today. And sometimes, despite your best efforts, a bug will linger for 
hours - or days - before you finally crack it.
This is all completely normal.
Debugging is part of the process, and it’s one of the most humbling and instructive aspects of 
software development. Think of it like solving a puzzle, except sometimes the pieces change shape 
as you're fitting them together. It's not uncommon to take one step forward only to stumble two steps 
back. But each stumble is an opportunity to learn something that will make you sharper the next time 
around.
Let’s say you’re trying to integrate a new authentication system. Y ou finally get the login flow working: 
users can sign in, sessions are created, and everything feels good. Then you notice your profile 
pages are broken. Turns out, the logic that checked if users were logged in was depending on the old 
session model. Now you're off fixing that, which leads to yet another issue with protected routes. This 
is how it goes. Fix one thing, and another area may break because of a dependency you didn’t 
realize was there.
It’s frustrating. But it’s also incredibly rewarding when you finally get it all working. That “aha!” moment 
after hours of debugging feels amazing like lifting a fog and suddenly seeing clearly.
If you're feeling stuck, consider reaching out to someone you know who works in tech. Seriously, buy 
them a coffee. Pick their brain. Share what you're building. Developers who’ve been through the wars 
of production software can offer you perspectives and shortcuts that no tutorial or forum can replace. 
Ask them about the worst bugs they’ve had to fix or how they manage complex systems. Y ou'll likely 
walk away with a better understanding of your own project and maybe even a new debugging 
strategy to try.
And if you’re the kind of person who thrives on structure, good news: you can bring that mindset into 
your workflow. Treat your software project like a checklist of small tasks. Some need to be done 
sequentially, while others can happen in parallel. Break big features down into smaller components 
like pages, buttons, API calls, validation rules. Ticking off those small wins one at a time not only 
builds momentum, but also helps you stay focused and avoid feeling overwhelmed.
Real-world example? A friend of mine was building a task management app with AI-generated 
reminders. It started off smooth with simple task creation, UI design, backend routes. Then came the 
intelligent reminder feature, where the app suggests when to do certain tasks based on user history. 

--- PDF PAGE 24 ---
Suddenly, timezone logic broke date formatting, which caused the reminders to fire at the wrong 
times. Fixing that broke the calendar view. Then the reminder email flow started firing twice. Every fix 
introduced a ripple effect. It took nearly a week to get it all behaving properly. But they learned more 
that week than they had in the previous month.
Y ou don’t need to be perfect, and you don’t need to solve everything immediately. Y ou just need to 
stay patient, curious, and willing to keep showing up.
Best Practices for Staying Sane and Steady During Debugging:
1. Expect setbacks—it’s part of the job.Don’t panic when something breaks. Assume it will, and be 
ready to investigate.
2. Keep your debugging process methodical.Work one problem at a time. Reproduce the issue. 
Log everything. Check assumptions.
3. Don’t debug in silence.Talk it through with a dev friend or even just out loud—it helps more than 
you think.
4. Use AI as a debugging buddy.Share logs, describe behavior, and ask the AI for likely causes or 
strategies to isolate the issue.
5. Talk to experienced developers.A coffee chat with someone who’s been there can give you 
shortcuts you won’t find online.
6. Lean into structure and task planning.Break your project into tiny, doable tasks and check them 
off as you go.
7. Be careful with “fixes” that create side effects.Always test after changes. What looks fixed in one 
place may be broken in another.
8. Celebrate small wins.Every bug you squash makes you a better builder.
